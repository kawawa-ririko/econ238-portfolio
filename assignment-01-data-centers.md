# 800,000 Gallons a Day Sounds Terrifying. It Tells Us Almost Nothing.

Imagine reading this headline:

New AI Data Center Will Consume 800,000 Gallons of Water Every Day

It sounds alarming. Maybe it should be.

But before deciding that the project is an environmental disaster, an economist should ask a very simple question:

Compared to what?

A large number can be completely true and still tell us surprisingly little.

Consider a large institution in Rochester. It occupies more than 700 acres and over 15 million square feet of buildings. It purchases roughly 225 million kilowatt-hours of electricity each year and millions of dekatherms of natural gas. Hundreds of millions of gallons of water move through its facilities, and it generates thousands of tons of waste.

That sounds like the description of an enormous industrial project.

It is actually the University of Rochester and UR Medicine.

This does not mean the University has no environmental impact. It clearly does. The point is that simply listing large numbers does not tell us whether an institution is socially harmful.

## Big Is Not the Same as Bad

Every large organization uses resources.

Hospitals use electricity. Universities use water. Factories occupy land. Farms consume water. Transportation systems create pollution.

Data centers are no different.

Colossus I, the large AI computing facility in Memphis, shows just how large modern data centers can be. A continuous 150-megawatt electricity load would use roughly 1.31 billion kilowatt-hours per year, almost six times the University of Rochester's annual grid electricity purchases.

That is a huge number.

But "huge" is not an economic conclusion.

The important question is whether the benefits created by that electricity justify its costs, and whether the company using it is actually paying the full cost of its decisions.

## The Real Question Is About Externalities

Suppose a data center buys electricity at a market price.

If that price reflects the full cost of generating and delivering the electricity, then using a lot of electricity is not automatically a problem.

But what if producing that electricity creates pollution that harms nearby residents?

What if a data center pumps groundwater and lowers nearby wells?

What if new transmission lines or power plants are partly paid for by other customers?

Those costs may fall on people who are not part of the transaction.

Economists call these costs externalities.

This is why a statement like "the data center uses 800,000 gallons of water per day" is incomplete.

If the water is abundant and the company pays its full opportunity cost, the number may be less concerning.

If the same water comes from a rapidly declining aquifer and nearby residents bear the cost of depletion, then the exact same number may represent a serious economic problem.

The number did not change.

The context did.

## Regulations Can Have Effects Beyond the Local Area

It is tempting to stop the analysis here and conclude that strict limits on data centers will solve the problem.

But economics asks another question:

And then what?

Suppose New York makes new data centers extremely difficult to build.

The immediate effect may be lower local electricity use, less water demand, and less pollution.

But the demand for computing does not disappear.

A company may simply build the same data center in another state.

If that state relies more heavily on fossil fuels or has weaker environmental rules, local pollution in New York may fall while total pollution rises.

On the other hand, stricter rules may push firms to develop more efficient chips, recycled-water cooling systems, or cleaner sources of power.

The important point is that policy changes incentives. We should judge policies by the consequences they actually produce, not only by their first visible effect.

## Ask Better Questions

When we see a headline about a giant data center, we should not ignore the number, but we should not stop at the number either.

Instead, we should ask:

- Where does the electricity come from?
- How scarce is the water?
- What else could those resources be used for?
- Who pays for new infrastructure?
- Who bears the pollution and congestion costs?
- What benefits does the facility produce?
- What happens if the project is blocked or moved elsewhere?

A data center consuming 800,000 gallons of water per day may be a serious environmental problem, or it may not be.

The number alone cannot tell us.

Economics begins where the frightening headline ends.
